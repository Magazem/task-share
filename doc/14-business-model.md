# Business Model

## Important principle

Do not monetize the first version aggressively.

The initial objective is proving the exchange.

## Possible revenue streams

### 1. Marketplace fee

Take a percentage of credit transactions.

Problem:

If credits are closed-loop and non-cash, a percentage fee can be
economically confusing.

### 2. Builder subscription

Charge builders for:

- private projects;
- advanced matching;
- analytics;
- task automation;
- higher limits;
- security controls.

This is likely cleaner.

### 3. Enterprise plan

Offer:

- private repositories;
- SSO;
- audit logs;
- contributor allowlists;
- policy controls;
- dedicated infrastructure.

### 4. Partner marketplace

Developer products can pay for distribution or provide subsidized
rewards.

### 5. Premium verification

Optional verification for:

- identity;
- expertise;
- organization;
- security training.

Avoid pay-to-win reputation.

### 6. Hosted execution

Later, provide sandboxed agent execution for users who do not want to
configure their own environment.

This can be usage-priced.

## What not to monetize initially

Avoid:

- selling user data;
- charging contributors to apply;
- pay-to-win ranking;
- hidden task fees;
- forced AI subscriptions.

## Long-term economic possibility

If the marketplace becomes liquid, the company could monetize the
infrastructure around the exchange rather than the labor itself:

- workflow;
- trust;
- security;
- matching;
- enterprise controls;
- agent infrastructure.

## Business model hypothesis

The most promising path may be:

> Free public/open-source exchange + paid private/enterprise
> infrastructure.

That keeps the network open while creating a conventional SaaS business
around higher-trust environments.
