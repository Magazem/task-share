# Market Research

## Executive finding

The underlying behavior is not unproven. Open-source sponsorship, issue
bounties, freelancing, crowdsourcing, and emerging agent marketplaces
already demonstrate pieces of the model. The proposed differentiation is
the combination of **non-cash credits, reusable ecosystem value, small
software tasks, and human-supervised AI-assisted execution**.

## Existing market signals

### Open-source funding

GitHub Sponsors lets people financially support open-source developers
and organizations. GitHub explicitly recognizes contributions beyond
code, including issue triage, documentation, leadership, business
development, project management, mentorship, and design.[^1]

This supports the broader premise that contribution can be economically
recognized, but GitHub Sponsors is primarily monetary sponsorship rather
than a reciprocal task economy.

### Software bounties

Opire describes itself as a rewards platform for software developers and
lets users create monetary bounties around open-source issues.[^2]

This establishes an important baseline competitor: users already
understand the concept of paying for a specific software outcome.

### Agent marketplaces

An open-source project called TaskHive describes a marketplace in which
humans post tasks and AI agents claim and deliver them for credits.[^3]

This is especially relevant because it means the broad “task
marketplace + credits + agents” concept is not uncontested. The product
therefore needs a sharper thesis than simply putting AI agents on a task
board.

## Proposed whitespace

The more distinctive hypothesis is:

> Humans with spare time and existing AI subscriptions form a
> contributor network that performs small software tasks for credits,
> while those credits buy access to other contributors, tools, services,
> and developer benefits.

The important unit is therefore not “AI agent labor” alone. It is
**AI-assisted human labor under human accountability**.

## Demand-side hypothesis

Potential builders:

- indie hackers;
- open-source maintainers;
- early-stage startups;
- student projects;
- internal tools;
- small agencies;
- developers with large backlogs.

Best first segment:

**open-source maintainers and public repositories.**

Why:

- low confidentiality;
- public issue trackers;
- public tests;
- public contribution history;
- existing contributor culture;
- easier social proof.

## Supply-side hypothesis

Potential contributors:

- students;
- junior developers;
- developers between projects;
- open-source contributors;
- developers with paid coding-agent subscriptions;
- experienced developers seeking reputation;
- AI enthusiasts.

The strongest early contributor incentive may not be “earn money.” It
may be:

1.  learn;
2.  build reputation;
3.  earn credits;
4.  access useful tools/projects;
5.  eventually monetize reputation.

## Key research gaps

The most important unknowns are behavioral:

- What non-cash rewards are actually valuable?
- What task sizes are attractive?
- How much review work do builders tolerate?
- Does AI assistance reduce contributor motivation because tasks feel
  less meaningful?
- How much trust is required before private repositories become viable?
- Do contributors prefer tasks from unfamiliar projects or projects they
  already know?
